# SECTION 0 — THE PHILOSOPHY OF ENEMIES

## 0.1 Why do enemies exist?

Because the harvest requires protection and the world still contains people who resist
being spent. Every enemy in Echoes of Eden is a **natural consequence of the Grimwood
Cycle's economy**: the Wardenry exists because forests became assets and assets need
guards [BI §IV.5]; the Devastators exist because people chained themselves to trees and
Edge budgets machines above men [BI §XIV.2]; the Mulchers exist because a dead world
still has an economy [BI §XIV.5]; Evergreen's defenses exist because a schedule this
holy cannot be interrupted. No enemy was invented by a villain. Each one began as a
solution — usually a reasonable one, usually celebrated [BI §0]. The player should be
able to trace every hostile silhouette back to a line item in the Accord Age's budget.

## 0.2 Why do they fight?

Each family fights for a different noun, and the nouns are the Central Question's four
answers made kinetic [BII §IV]: the Wardenry fights for **order** ("between the work
and harm" — they believe they are the restraint); the machines fight for **the
institution** (threat-tables executing policy without content); the Mulchers fight for
**the margin** (transactional, both ways, break off when costed); Evergreen's defenses
fight for **the schedule** (continuity of the one devotion). Nobody fights the player
personally — and that impersonality IS the horror the combat must carry. The Wardens
would rather the player left. The machines do not know the player exists as anything
but a classification. The design's enemy-facing rule: **no hatred anywhere on the
battlefield** — only procedure, price, and devotion.

## 0.3 Why haven't they disappeared? What keeps them alive?

The grid keeps them alive — literally. The Wardenry is the largest armed force on the
continent because the Republic's own army was out-budgeted and emotionally garrisoned
to Edge after 2064 [BI §IX.1]. Wardens stay for wages, medical coverage (Dez's
arithmetic — [BIII §8]), and crews they love. The machines persist because scrapping a
nine-figure asset over three deaths was never on any agenda [BII §IV.2]. The Mulchers
persist because everything has an economy, including collapse. Evergreen persists
because it is funded inside Edge's own blind spot [BII §IX R4]. Enemies in Eden are
not respawning abstractions: each one is *paid for*, and the paying is visible
(liveries, maintenance states, permit paperwork).

## 0.4 How do they relate to the collapse?

They are its maintenance crew. The Quiet is the true antagonist [BI §XVII.5], and every
combat faction is one of its organs: the Wardenry keeps the withdrawal orderly, the
machines make it scale, the Mulchers recycle its leavings, Evergreen archives its
victims. Combat in this game is therefore never "against evil" — it is against **the
apparatus of a spending that everyone benefits from**, which is why the encounters
must never feel righteous and always feel *costly* [B0 §2.1].

## 0.5 How do they relate to architecture?

Enemies are tenants of the same civilization the player reads [BVI §0.2]: Wardens
patrol like the colonnades they walk — metronomic, interval-regular; machines berth in
the industrial kit's organ-pipe gantries and drip their oil confessions [BIV §7];
Mulchers infest thresholds and tide-lines (half in one element, half in another, like
Brack); Evergreen's Custodians are *fixtures* — the building fighting back. Encounter
placement obeys Book VI: every fight sits where the story bleeds, under something
enormous, and leaves procedure behind (cordon tape, tags, serials — never gore)
**LAW**.

## 0.6 How do they relate to memory?

Every enemy is somebody the world will have to remember. Wardens carry name-tapes and
crew talismans; their deaths leave paperwork the world files (the "filed" AI state —
Book V §6.6 — is also a *memorial* system: the cordon that appears where the player
fought is the world grieving in its only language). The machines are memory's
inversion: they retain everything (threat-tables, logged classifications) and remember
nothing (no grudges, no fear, no learning beyond the table) — perfect records, zero
memory, which is the Accord Age's own pathology in a chassis [B0 §1].

## 0.7 How do they relate to the protagonist?

They are his biography armed. The Wardenry is his family and his muscle memory — the
player fights bone-white silhouettes wearing their old kit **LAW** [BIV.5 §13 loss of
identity], and the prologue makes the player BE one first [BIII §16]. The D-2 is his
paperwork executing. Harrow is his parent in doctrine. The Mulchers are his supply
chain gone feral (Coil's people, transactional cousins). Evergreen is his hometown's
other son, automated. The roster rule: **no enemy exists without a relationship to
Asher's past or the cell's present** — an enemy the story cannot introduce is an enemy
the game does not ship.

## 0.8 How do they change across the story?

The world escalates by *institutional logic*, never by power inflation [Book V §6.8]:
Act I — patrols doing jobs (the player is a nuisance in someone's sector); Act II —
the institution notices (named rotations, marksmen, hazard crews, and finally the
taboo: armed Wardens in a Standing Hall, once, forever [Lock §20.9]); Act III — the
apparatus at home (Steelgate's honest fortress; then Evergreen, where the enemy stops
being people entirely and becomes a building with a timetable). In parallel the
Wardenry *decays morally on camera*: after Ch.6 (the page, Sela, Dez's defection) the
player fights an institution that has publicly lost its conscience — barks change,
morale states loosen, and by Ch.9 some Wardens hesitate **[NEW CANON — the Hesitation
state, §9.6]**. The machines never change. That contrast is the argument.

# SECTION 1 — THE FACTION BIBLE

*Nine factions. Four field combatants against the player (Wardenry, Edge machines
under Edge's entry, Mulchers, Evergreen), five never-combatant (Edge civil, Sowers,
Ledger, Bright Hour, Mulcher-neutral wings, Registry/Republic — consolidated below).
All facts trace to Lock §6/§17; new detail is organizational texture only.*

## 1.1 EDGE CORPORATION

- **Official name:** Edge Corporation (formerly Edge Holdings, renamed 2038).
- **History:** logistics conglomerate → Grimwood's backer (2034) → the energy monopoly
  that ended fossil fuels and drank two-thirds of Earth's wild biomass [Lock §4].
- **Purpose:** continuity of the grid. **Philosophy:** "the lights stay on."
  **Ideology:** continuity at any cost; survival now, priced in everything later.
- **Leadership:** an unseen Board; Dr. Beck Grimwood (Chief Scientist, sanctified
  founder-mind, arrested 2071); Director Coren Voss (operations; controls Edge 2072).
- **Population:** hundreds of thousands of employees; the arcology professional class
  lives inside its benefit [BI §XII].
- **Hierarchy:** Board → Directorates (Operations/Voss, Science/Grimwood, Concessions)
  → regional administrations → the Wardenry (enforcement arm, §1.2).
- **Culture:** competence-worship, "clean" as the highest compliment, violence dressed
  as procedure **LAW** [Lock §20.8]; grief managed by translation-vocabulary.
- **Technology:** Converters, Vaults, Devastators, mirror-farms, mag-freight [Lock §10].
- **Architecture:** Gravity Style at its coldest; Steelgate; the district that lies
  [BIV §8]. **Visual language:** bone-white `#E8E4DA` + safety amber `#D9A226`,
  machine-formed pour, tract-serial grotesk. **Symbols:** the sunrise (line breaking a
  circle's rim). **Palette:** bone/amber on concrete grey; red only on Devastator
  service bands **LAW**.
- **Communication style:** manifests, permits, compliance phrases; the passive voice as
  a weapon ("the crew was reclassified").
- **Relationships:** funds the Assembly's third and the Bright Hour discreetly; split
  internally Grimwood vs. Voss [Lock §6]; to the protagonist: former employer, current
  landlord of his prosthetic (property clause, trackable).
- **Status 2071:** monopoly. **Future:** Voss's Edge — dreamless and durable; the
  franchise regards this as worse [BII §IV.3].

## 1.2 THE WARDENRY — *the enemy family in bone-white*

- **Official name:** the Wardenry ("asset protection contractors" in law).
- **History:** founded 2050s from ruined ranger services and army leavings during the
  Quiet Creep [Lock §4]; author of its creed: Cmdr. Edda Harrow.
- **Purpose:** asset protection — anti-poaching, anti-sabotage, evictions, harvest
  escort. **Philosophy/creed:** **"BETWEEN THE WORK AND HARM"** — stenciled in every
  barracks, believed sincerely **LAW** [BI §XIV.2]. **Ideology:** order as mercy; the
  thin disciplined line that keeps the harvest from becoming a massacre.
- **Leadership:** regional commanders (Harrow, Concession III, until Ch.6); below the
  Board's Concession administrators. **Population:** the continent's largest armed
  force [Lock §6]. **Hierarchy [NEW CANON — service structure]:** Recruit → Warden →
  Warden First Class (Asher's rank) → Corporal (Dez's) → Watch Sergeant → Watch
  Commander → Regional Commander. Field organization: the **rotation** (a scheduled
  patrol circuit), the **escort** (convoy/crew protection detail), the **watch**
  (static site security), the **response** (breach/raid element, post-2064).
- **Culture:** crew loyalty above doctrine, doctrine above Edge (they believe the
  creed harder than the corporation does); boots inspected, not faces; talismans in
  lockers (the child's tree drawing [BI §I]); "steady work and a crew" [BI §XIV.2].
- **Technology:** ballistic arms (white/grey composite, amber charge-windows —
  status indicators, never energy **LAW**); Arsenal-pattern refurbished weapons
  [Book V §4]; survey drones in support.
- **Architecture:** barracks in Concession industrial kit; checkpoint furniture.
- **Visual language:** bone-white composite plate over storm-grey duty fleece, amber
  signal panels; hazard variants in yellow suits [Lock §17]. Silhouette family: **pale
  verticals with amber breaks** [BI §XVI].
- **Military structure:** pairs by doctrine (nobody patrols alone); squads of 4–6;
  escalation drills (§9.2); marksman sections; hazard crews (harvest-site ops).
- **Symbols:** the creed stencil; unit chevrons; name-tapes. **Palette:** bone + amber
  on grey. **Communication:** radio grammar, compliance phrases, incident reports in
  longhand.
- **Relationship to protagonist:** his family for twelve years; they still call him
  "Amber Six" on the hunt because it still works on him [BIII §1.1].
- **Relationship to world:** loved by its own, feared by the provinces, publicly
  shamed after Ch.6 (the page) — morale broken on camera [Lock §6].
- **Status 2071:** intact, then fracturing. **Future:** Harrow's diaspora — ex-Wardens
  who follow her out [Lock §19.7].

## 1.3 EDGE MACHINES — *the appliance that is also the argument*

Not a faction in the social sense: a **fleet**. Owned by Edge, tasked by
administrators, exactly as moral as their owners **LAW** (no AGI, ever [Lock §3.5]).
History: pollinator/survey drones (2040s) → Devastator program (Grimwood, 2050s):
D-1 fielded 2058, D-2 2065, D-3000 2070 [Lock §4]. Purpose: harvest at scale;
"contested biome" operations. Philosophy: none — **that is the point**; threat-tables
execute policy. Visual language: industrial, blocky, safety-liveried — Edge paints its
violence like its forklifts **LAW** [BI §VII.3]; unit chevrons + red service band
(never eyes — machines are appliances, never faces **LAW**). Communication:
classification barks (synthesized dispatch phrases, not "speech"), spotlight color
states. Relationship to protagonist: one of them took his hand while correctly
executing his own paperwork — the two-layer Amber account **LAW** [Lock §21-D].
Status: D-2 fleet + first D-3000s [Lock §10]. Future: Voss inherits the fleet and the
archive [Lock §19.3].

## 1.4 THE MULCHERS — *the economy with a crowbar*

Official name: none (syndicate slang; they file as "salvage cooperatives"). History:
Driftmark and Belt salvage crews professionalized by the Quiet's economics [BI §XIV.5].
Purpose: dead timber, Vault-stripping, permit forgery, seed theft when the price is
right. Philosophy: **transactional both ways** — they sell to Sowers and inform to
Wardens in the same week. Ideology: the margin. Leadership: crew-bosses and
tide-captains; no center. Population: thousands across the coast and Belt. Hierarchy:
crew (4–8) under a captain; federated by debt, not command. Culture: debts are sacred,
flags are not; gauge-fobs as paid-debt jewelry [BIII §12]; Coil's origin culture.
Technology: cutting rigs, leech kits, tide-barges, mixed salvage arms. Architecture:
none built — thresholds infested (tide-lockers, tower squats). Visual language: mixed
salvage kit — Edge castoffs re-edged, oilskin, verdigris; silhouette family: **bulk
and asymmetry** (against the Wardenry's uniform verticals). Symbols: crew marks
chalked, not flown. Palette: slate, oxide, verdigris; no allowance color — they wear
everyone's leavings. Communication: barter cant, tide-tables, prices before pronouns.
Military structure: none — work crews that fight like work crews. Relationship to
protagonist: suppliers (the Jack, the Tide come from their world) and occasional
opponents at seed vaults; to Coil: family, complicatedly. Relationship to factions:
neutral zone, permanently for rent [Lock §6]. Status 2071: business as usual. Future:
the valley's legality is bad for salvage; they'll adapt — they always do.
**Combat identity LAW [Lock §17]: transactional — Mulcher combatants BREAK OFF when
costed; no Mulcher fights to the death, ever; every Mulcher encounter can end in
withdrawal.**

## 1.5 PROJECT EVERGREEN (GRIMWOOD'S STAFF & DEFENSES) — *devotion automated*

Official name: Project Evergreen (publicly "closed-loop photosynthesis farm"). History:
Grimwood's design era post-2069, built inside Edge's budget without full Board sanction
[Lock §8 hidden element]. Purpose: the whole-canopy intake and the archive — a
Returning performed on the biosphere **LAW** [Lock §12]. Philosophy: banking is love.
Leadership: Grimwood; below him the Republic's best ecologists, archivists, and Bright
Hour registrars — **believers, not thugs, and NEVER combatants** [BII §III.1; several
defend the position to the player in optional dialogue]. Population: a few hundred
staff, 1,100 m down. Culture: monastic-clinical; index cards; the recitation.
Technology: the array, intake stations, the governor; **the Custodian system** (§3.4)
— automated station-denial defenses. Architecture: the inverted Standing Hall [Lock
§11]. Visual language: white/teal lab-industrial [Lock §17]; light from below.
Symbols: Grimwood's thickened double rule. Palette: machine-white, teal charge-lines,
bone. Communication: schedules; the docent's voice. Relationship to protagonist: the
other son of Halden's Ford, at scale. Status 2071: runs once; reversed. Future: the
archive — spent of charge, intact of record, in Voss's inventory [Lock §19.3].

## 1.6 THE NON-COMBATANT FACTIONS (locked out of the enemy roster forever)

**The Sowers** (the player's own faction — cells, planter/demolition wings, "a dead
Sower plants nothing"); **the Ledger** (clergy never bless violence; parishes shelter
[Lock §20.13]); **the Bright Hour** (sincere, never mocked, never fought); **the
Registry** (a record, not a resistance); **the Meridian Republic** (courts and
assembly; its small army never takes the field against the player). Full canon: Lock
§6. They appear in this book only as the *social terrain* enemies move through — and
as the hard wall of Rule 16: none of these people may ever be a target, a hostage-
puzzle, or collateral the player can cause **LAW**.

## 1.7 Harvest crews — **[NEW CANON — ruling: permanently non-combatant]**

Lock §17 left "armed variant TBD-BIII"; Book III added none. **This book rules the
question closed: harvest crews are civilians, permanently, franchise-wide.** They flee,
shelter, and carry the game's most uncomfortable truth — the people doing the
harvesting are workers with thermoses [BII §XII P]. The Amber Incident's three dead
were exactly these people; arming them would retroactively cheapen the game's founding
grief. Logged in PROJECT_STATE.md.

# SECTION 3 — THE ENEMY FAMILIES (FULL TREATMENTS)

## 3.1 FAMILY A — THE WARDENRY

- **History/origin:** §1.2. **Evolution across the campaign:** patrols → named
  rotations hunting "Amber Six" → the taboo raid → Steelgate's watch → fracture
  (defections, the Hesitation, Harrow's diaspora).
- **Purpose in the design:** the human cost of order — the enemy the player used to
  be. **Narrative symbolism:** the creed's middle collapsing; restraint discovering it
  was never the variable [BII §IV.1].
- **Visual language:** bone-white composite over storm-grey, amber signal panels;
  plate arcs readable (aimed-fire skill target [Book V §3.5]); wear per Edge law —
  controlled patina on parade units, chalking and honey-fade on forgotten ones (Dez's
  grammar [BIII §8]); name-tapes and unit chevrons; NO faces behind glass — visors are
  matte, humanity carried by voice and posture instead **[NEW CANON — visor law: the
  player must never see a Warden's eyes in combat; faces belong to the named cast]**.
- **Movement:** doctrine incarnate — interval-regular patrol gaits, corner drills,
  pair-cover leapfrog; they move like the colonnades they guard [§0.5]. Under fire:
  trained bounds between cover, suppression-and-flank by the manual (§9.3).
- **Combat style:** procedure. Escalation drills (§9.2): announce → posture → fire.
  Semi-auto discipline (their C-9s burst, never spray — mix law [Book V 4-A]).
- **Materials:** worn composite (chalking edges, UV-fade by posting), storm fleece,
  canvas rigs — every plate answers "what motion made this?" **LAW**.
- **Audio:** compliance phrases (the game's most chilling dialogue — office language
  at gunpoint: "Comply and this is a citation." / "You are being recorded."); radio
  grammar; boot cadence in pairs; plate-clatter on sprints. Post-Ch.6: the phrases
  fray — some Wardens stop announcing (§5), some announce too much (the Hesitation).
- **Animation:** parade-straight idles decaying by act; weapon-index low on patrol,
  high on alert; the salute-check gesture when passing creed stencils (ambient
  characterization — build it once, place it everywhere).
- **AI philosophy:** §9 — the five-state grammar plus doctrine layers; crews preserve
  crews (they drag wounded out under cover; the player watches the creed practiced).
- **Relationship to architecture:** checkpoints at thresholds, rotations on the
  colonnade grid, marksmen on the skyline law's verticals. **To other enemies:** they
  resent the machines (the D-2 took THEIR people too — barks carry it); they price
  Mulchers as weather.

## 3.2 FAMILY B — EDGE MACHINES

- **History/origin/evolution:** §1.3; the Devastator line D-1 (2058) → D-2 (2065) →
  D-3000 (2070) is the institution's answer scaling as restraint fails.
- **Purpose in the design:** institutional emptiness — combat as encounter with a
  policy. **Narrative symbolism:** perfect record, zero memory (§0.6); the forklift at
  the end of history [BII §IV.4].
- **Visual language:** industrial, blocky, safety-liveried; chevrons + red service
  band (the game's ONLY combat red **LAW**); no eyes, no faces, no anthropomorphism
  **LAW**; appliance-honest cable sag and oil confession.
- **Movement:** task-priority pathing — machines move like schedules, not hunters;
  they will walk PAST the player to reach a sapling (sapling-priority canon [Lock
  §17]) and that indifference is scarier than pursuit.
- **Combat style:** threat-table postures, telegraphed state changes (the Assessor's
  Read reads them [Book V §2.14 Tier 3]); no flanking, no fear, no adaptation beyond
  table switches — their tactics are POLICY, legible and remorseless.
- **Materials:** matte alloy shells, ceramic-composite plates chalking at true
  corners, teal charge-lines, hydraulic weep.
- **Audio:** servo grammar per mark (D-2 quadruped gait = four-count industrial
  knock); classification barks (synthesized dispatch: "UNAUTHORIZED PRESENCE.
  CLASSIFYING."); capacitor whine; **machines get no melody LAW** [B0 §12] —
  percussion from sampled machine parts only. The sigh when they harvest **LAW**.
- **Animation:** mechanical honesty — weight, spin-up delays, no snappy game-feel
  cheats; **death = power-down mid-gesture LAW** (the D-2's locked defeat generalized:
  machines never "die," they stop being funded).
- **AI philosophy:** tables, not minds (§9.5). **Relationship to architecture:**
  berths, rails, gantries — machines live where the building expects them; a machine
  off its infrastructure is an EVENT (the D-2 standing in the acre reads as violation
  because the level taught its habitat).

## 3.3 FAMILY C — THE MULCHERS

- **History/origin:** §1.4. **Evolution:** none — permanence is their point; the same
  crews before, during, and after the campaign.
- **Purpose in the design:** moral texture — combat that can END WITHOUT KILLING;
  proof the world is an economy, not a war. **Narrative symbolism:** how reasonable
  survival is; Brack's whole arc in crowd form.
- **Visual language:** asymmetric salvage bulk — mixed plate, oilskin, verdigris
  re-edging, tide-hooks; crew marks chalked on shoulders; silhouettes deliberately
  UN-uniform (the anti-Wardenry).
- **Movement:** work-crew economy — they move like cargo handlers, take cover like
  men who know loads, retreat like professionals cutting losses.
- **Combat style:** opportunist — they shoot to drive off, not to finish; they take
  the seeds and GO; sustained resistance re-prices the job (§9.7 cost-morale).
- **Materials/audio:** chain through cleats, oilskin creak, barter cant shouted
  ("Not worth it! Not at this rate!"— morale as price, audible); the Tide's oceanic
  boom is their weapon voice [Book V §4.2 W3].
- **Animation:** heavy-handling poses, tool-first grips (cutters fight holding
  cutters); their retreat animation set is as rich as their attack set —
  **withdrawal is their signature move LAW**.
- **AI philosophy:** §9.7. **Relationship to architecture:** thresholds and
  tide-lines; they never hold ground — ground is inventory, not territory.

## 3.4 FAMILY D — EVERGREEN (THE CUSTODIAN SYSTEM) **[NEW CANON — unit family]**

- **History/origin:** Grimwood's defense philosophy applied to his life's work: no
  guards with guns near the archive (guards improvise; improvisation harms
  collections) — instead the building itself denies interruption. Designed by the
  same hands as the array; poured into Verge's shell.
- **Purpose in the design:** the final family removes PEOPLE from the enemy roster
  entirely — the last hour's opposition is a devotion running on rails, so the

# SECTION 5 — ELITE ENEMIES

## 5.1 Why elites exist

Because institutions have seniority, and seniority is the honest form of "harder
enemy." An Eden elite is never a palette-swap, never larger, never glowing **LAW** —
it is a *veteran*: the same doctrine executed by someone who has stopped needing the
manual. Elites exist to break the player's learned solutions (the confidence plateau's
counterweight [BIV.5 §2.9]) and to carry the fiction's saddest truth: the best people
in the Wardenry are the ones the creed will spend hardest.

## 5.2 How players identify them

By **biography, not badges**: wear tells rank (§3.1) — resprayed plate arcs, mended
straps, tally-notched furniture, the sun-map of postings; by **movement** — the manual
perfected reads differently at 100 m (shorter bounds, no wasted checks); by **audio**
— veterans announce LESS (the compliance phrases fray to single words; silence
outranks speech in this army as in this game). The Read (Tier 2+) annotates them in
the journal ("First Class. Careful."). No UI marker, no health bar difference **LAW**.

## 5.3 How encounters change

Elites edit the squad's grammar: baits refused, flanks pre-aimed, drills compressed;
the squad's doctrine state (§9.4) degrades more slowly while they stand. The counter
is Book V's thesis — preparation and position, not caliber: elites punish improvisation
and reward the read-route.

## 5.4 How fear increases · how music changes

Fear rises by comprehension **LAW** [BIV.5 §11]: the player recognizes competence the
way professionals do — small tells, early. And by *affection displaced*: elites are
loved (partner barks, name-use under fire), so fighting them costs. Music: NOTHING
changes **LAW** — no elite sting, no layer shift [B0 §13.91]. The mix does the work:
elite spaces run 2 dB quieter ambient (the room holding its breath) and their audio
presence is *reduction* — fewer footsteps heard, fewer words. In Eden, silence is
rank.

# SECTION 8 — THE FINAL BOSS: THE EVERGREEN ARRAY (AND THE MAN STANDING IN IT)

## 8.1 Why this is the final boss

Because the campaign's argument ends here and nowhere else can hold it. Every prior
boss was one answer to the Central Question wearing armor; the Array is the question
itself, built — a machine that is also a funeral that is also a faith that is also a
father's ledger re-inscribed at biosphere scale [BII §III]. It cannot be a bigger
D-3000, because the campaign has already proven bigness empty (Ch.9); it cannot be a
duel, because the player has already been disowned (Ch.6); it cannot be killable,
because Grimwood is never killable **LAW** and because the game's thesis is that
authorship, not destruction, is what the Accord Age abolished [BII §IX]. The final
boss is **a schedule the player denies and a lever the player holds** — mechanically
distinct from every fight before it, and only meaningful because of every fight
before it.

## 8.2 The multi-stage structure

1. **THE DESCENT** (the 90 seconds, cable-song and breathing — the boss's true
   introduction: its silence rising to meet the cage) →
2. **THE ANTECHAMBER** (the recitation overheard; the reactive dialogue node; the
   staff who cannot be fought **LAW** — the game disarms the player's category of
   "enemy" one room before the end) →
3. **THE RINGS** (station-denial combat down the gallery levels: Custodian frames,
   Steward arms, the intake countdowns — the docent narrating his life's work to the
   person dismantling its evening **LAW**; difficulty peaks HERE, at maximum
   sympathy [BIV.5 §14]) →
4. **THE REACH** (the last ring holds no combat **[NEW CANON — staging]**: the final
   approach to the governor is undefended, because Grimwood never believed anyone
   who understood the work would stop it — the walk is the fight) →
5. **THE TEN SECONDS** (the held input **LAW**; the anti-sigh building; the carved
   sentence above the lever; Grimwood reciting; the flood).

## 8.3 Why this fight could exist nowhere else

Its arena is the style's theological end-state (the inverted Standing Hall **LAW**);
its "health bar" is a timetable; its voice is the campaign's warmest; its defenses
refuse to kill **LAW** (§3.4 — Evergreen preserves, even now); and its defeat
condition is the game's entire progression system arriving in one hand [Book V §5.5:
the Reversal Grip — the finale uses no ability the player didn't grow]. Remove any
locked element — the chamber, the docent, the schedule, the lever — and the
encounter collapses into a boss fight, which is precisely what it must never be.

## 8.4 How it changes the player's understanding of the universe

R5 lands mid-fight [Lock §15]: Evergreen is a Returning — the villain's plan is the
faith's holiest rite performed sincerely at civilization scale. The player enters
believing they are stopping a harvest and leaves knowing they interrupted a funeral
to argue with the officiant about what deserves to live. The almost-agreement is the
mechanic: the fight is TUNED so the player is losing the argument while winning the
schedule — and the ten seconds at the governor are long enough to change your mind,
which is why they are ten seconds **LAW**. After the flood: "One valley." / "One
more than you were leaving." The universe re-prices from apocalypse-to-be-prevented
into *account-to-be-corrected* — the franchise's whole future lives in that
re-pricing.

## 8.5 Production keys

Emotionally meaningful: verified by exit-word "acceptance" **LAW**. Narratively
inevitable: caused twice, like everything [BII §0] — the world's clock and Grimwood's
faith both arrive here. Architecturally monumental: Book VI §12.5's build document
governs. Visually unforgettable: the game's single chromatic release (white → the
sanctioned green flood **LAW**); the only downward reveal. Mechanically distinct:
schedule-denial + the held input; no HP anywhere on screen **LAW**. Grimwood's
performance: the docent — warmth, never menace **LAW**; mixed nearest of all sounds;
his theme resolves to true tempo at the flip, corrected, not silenced [BIII §2.9].

# SECTION 9 — AI PHILOSOPHY

*No implementation — behavioral law. The base grammar is Book V §6.6 (binding); this
section completes it per family. The universal rule: **AI is characterization** — every
behavior must be attributable to a doctrine, a price, a table, or a schedule; "because
it's harder" is not a reason any behavior ships.*

## 9.1 The five states (all families)

**Unaware → Curious (investigate on procedure, 20–40 s) → Engaged → Searching
(last-known-position, 45–90 s) → FILED** — the institutional giving-up: cordon
posture, radio-in, resume, with a PERMANENT logged world-change (next rotation +1,
new chalk, cordon serials) **LAW**. Enemies never psychically track **LAW**; all
detection is sense-honest (§14).

## 9.2 Awareness & escalation (Wardenry)

Escalation drills are the Wardenry's soul [Lock §17]: ANNOUNCE (compliance phrase,
distance-scaled) → POSTURE (weapon index, cover claim) → FIRE. The drill is skippable
only by the player firing first — the institution never shoots without paperwork
prepared. Awareness is trained but human: cone-of-attention narrows on task, pairs
cover arcs, torches announce their bearers.

## 9.3 Coordination, suppression, flanking (Wardenry)

By the manual: suppression is a ROLE (one gun talks while pairs bound); flanking
follows architecture's own lanes (they know their buildings — the player reads the
same lanes via the Read, the fight as competing literacies); crossfire is built
deliberately and audibly (called, countable). Coordination quality = commander state
(§9.4). **Retreat:** doctrine-approved and dignified — bounding withdrawal, wounded
dragged, cordon dropped; the Wardenry preserves crews **LAW** ("a creed, practiced").

## 9.4 Command & the org chart in combat

While a Sergeant/Commander stands: full doctrine. Commander routed/down: the CHECK
(two-beat all-stop), then degraded doctrine (shorter bounds, broken crossfire,
earlier withdrawal thresholds). Marksman down: patrols lose lane data (bolder player
movement reads as safe). The player dismantles ENCOUNTERS by dismantling structure —
never by attrition alone.

## 9.5 The machines: tables, not minds

No fear, no morale, no adaptation, no memory beyond the log **LAW** (no AGI).
Machines telegraph by POSTURE (state changes are physical, readable, Read-annotated);
they prioritize by policy (sapling-first, asset-first — legible and horrifying);
they never search creatively (lattice sweeps, schedule resumption); they never
retreat — they are RECALLED (dispatch decides, audibly). Environmental interaction:
task-first (a crawler clears debris to keep its route; the player can weaponize the
diligence).

## 9.6 Fear, morale & the Hesitation **[NEW CANON — post-Ch.6 state]**

Human morale is doctrinal, not hit-point-based: it degrades by structure loss (§9.4),
by cost (Mulchers §9.7), and — after the page circulates (Ch.6) — by CONSCIENCE: some
Wardens enter the Hesitation, a readable half-beat before firing on a clear shot, a
compliance phrase restarted, a torch lowered. It is rare (tuned ~1 in 6 post-Ch.6
line Wardens), never exploitable as a stealth mechanic, and never spoken — the
institution's doubt made visible at the exact size doubt actually has. Breach
Wardens and elites never hesitate; that is also information.

## 9.7 The Mulcher price engine

Morale = job price: every Mulcher encounter runs an audible ledger (crew losses,
time, foam walls, the Tide-Captain's presence) against the take. Cost exceeds take →
the withdrawal whistle → they LEAVE, professionally, retrieving their fallen if
allowed. The player can win Mulcher fights by economics alone **LAW** — pressure,
denial, and patience end encounters without a body count, and the design rewards
noticing.

## 9.8 Investigation & memory (world-side)

Investigation is procedure (chalk, torch, radio, cordon); memory belongs to the
WORLD, not the unit **LAW**: the filed state writes to the world ledger (rotations
harden, routes re-chalk, drones re-task), so consequence persists without
psychic AI. Adaptation across the campaign is authored (Act escalation §0.8), never
runtime learning — the institution changes by policy, like institutions.

# SECTION 16 — THE CREATURE DIRECTOR'S MANIFESTO

*To every creature artist, combat designer, and AI engineer who joins this project
after us.*

**Enemies are characters** because in this world they are people — or they are the
property of people — and nothing else. You will never design a monster here. You will
design a woman three years from pension walking a rotation she no longer believes in,
a drone that files the player under a serial number, a salvage crew that would rather
be paid than win, and a machine so innocent of malice that killing it feels like
vandalizing a funeral. Give every unit what you would give a named character: a job,
a wage, a wear biography, and one gesture that is theirs. The player will shoot at
silhouettes; they will remember the ones that were somebody.

**Combat tells stories** because every fight in Eden is an argument between answers
to one question. When a Warden drags his partner out of your fire lane, the creed is
speaking. When a Mulcher crew whistles withdrawal because you made the job too
expensive, the economy is speaking. When the D-2 steps past the player to reach a
sapling, the whole Accord Age is speaking. Choreograph the argument. If a fight
cannot say anything, cut it — the silence will say more.

**Bosses represent ideas** — this is law, not aspiration. Order. The institution. The
work. Continuity. Each boss is one answer armed, and each defeat is the answer
failing in front of the player in its own vocabulary: the duel resigns, the machine
powers down mid-gesture unaware, the graph falls on the bodyguard, the funeral is
reversed by one held hand. Never let a boss become a health bar with a skin. The
exit-interview word is your acceptance test: if players say "epic," you have built
the wrong fight, no matter how good it felt.

**Visual identity matters** because this game's enemies must be readable at 100
meters in fog and attributable at one glance to a civilization's budget. Bone and
amber is a wage. Yellow is a workplace. Salvage is a margin. Porcelain and teal is a
devotion. Red is a service band on a machine, and nothing else, ever. Spend wear like
the writers spend words: every scuff is a shift somebody worked.

**Memorable enemies outlive graphics.** Nobody remembers polycounts; they remember
the enemy that hesitated, the crew that carried its dead out, the machine that
stopped being funded rather than defeated, the commander who emptied her sidearm
into ash and walked out of the war. Build behaviors worth remembering and the art
will be remembered with them.

**How to evaluate every future enemy:** ask, in order — (1) What budget line pays
for it? (2) What noun does it fight for? (3) What does it look like at 100 m in fog?
(4) What is its one gesture? (5) What does killing it cost the player's chest?
Five answers: build it. Any silence: bring the spade.

*Still here.*

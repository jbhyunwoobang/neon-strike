# ECHOES OF EDEN
## BOOK I — THE UNIVERSE BIBLE
### Edition One · Foundational Canon · 2026

*This document is the root of the franchise. Every book, sprint, level, cutscene, prop label,
and line of dialogue written after it must be consistent with it. Where later books contradict
Book I, Book I wins until formally amended. It is written to be handed to a development team:
environment artists should be able to build from Section VI alone, writers from Sections IV
and XII, graphic designers from Section X.*

*Canon sources: the permanent reference board ("Game Project – reference"), which establishes
the title treatment, the protagonist Asher Forester, Edge Corp, Dr. Beck Grimwood, the
Converter, the Devastator series, the Bioharvest Vault, the Sowers, and the Battle of
Steelgate. This bible deepens that material; it never overwrites it.*

---

# 0. HOW TO READ THIS WORLD

Echoes of Eden is not a story about the end of the world. It is a story about a world that
learned to pay for its life with its life, and considered the arrangement reasonable.

Nothing in this universe was invented by a villain. Every terrible thing in it began as a
solution, usually a beautiful one, usually celebrated. The reader should come away from this
document unable to point to the exact year everything went wrong — because there is no such
year. There is only a long corridor of sensible decisions, each opening onto the next, until
the people walking it realized the corridor had no doors and the light behind them had gone
out.

The tone is not despair. It is *aftermath and stubborn green*. Wonder, melancholy, awe,
loneliness, and a hope that has to be planted one seed at a time.

---

# I. THE CENTRAL QUESTION

> **"If survival requires spending the living world, what exactly is being kept alive?"**

Every system in this universe is an answer to that question, offered by someone who believed
it.

Edge Corporation answers: *civilization is being kept alive — the grid, the hospitals, the
cities, the nine hundred million people who did not choose to be born into a depleted world
and deserve light and heat anyway.* Edge is not lying. That is what makes Edge dangerous.

Dr. Beck Grimwood answers: *knowledge is being kept alive.* The Grimwood Cycle was the single
greatest thermodynamic discovery of the century, and its author cannot conceive of a world
that would be better off not knowing it. To Grimwood, the forests were always going to be
spent by someone; better that they be spent *efficiently*, by him, on the record.

The Meridian government answers: *order is being kept alive,* and signs the Concessions.

The old faith, the Ledger, answers: *nothing is kept alive this way. Life is not a quantity
that can be moved from one column to another. A ledger that only ever debits is not an
economy; it is an obituary written slowly.*

And the Sowers — Asher Forester among them — answer with the only argument that has ever
moved the needle of history: they stop asking the question rhetorically. They go out into the
grey stillwood with seed cases and stolen Converters run in reverse, and they make the
question physical: *watch. This is what "alive" means. It means this.*

The player should never be handed this question in a menu. They should find it in the world:
in a cathedral-sized turbine hall built on the bones of a forest, in a child's drawing of a
tree taped inside a Warden barracks locker, in the sound difference between a level set in
the last living canopy (birdsong, insects, wind with texture) and a level set in the
stillwood (a silence so engineered the player checks their audio settings).

# II. EXECUTIVE PREMISE

**Logline.** 2071. Energy is abundant, the world is quiet, and the quiet is spreading. Asher
Forester — a decorated enforcer for the corporation that harvests the life out of forests —
loses his hand to the machine he served, and his certainty with it. Recruited by the Sowers,
a scattered resistance that plants by night what Edge spends by day, he turns the
corporation's own harvest tools into weapons and walks back into the concrete cathedrals he
helped build, to switch the world's account from debit to seed.

**Era and place.** The game is set in 2071, primarily within the **Meridian Republic** — a
fictionalized North American federation — in and around the arcology-capital **Cadence** and
the vast harvesting Concessions west of it. The world is Earth, one generation from now,
recognizable in its bones and estranged in its surfaces.

**State of the world.** Not post-apocalyptic in the cinematic sense — no craters, no
wandering hordes. Something quieter and worse: a *post-verdant* world. Two-thirds of the
planet's wild biomass has been converted to charge. The countryside is standing grey timber,
mineral-still, preserved like a museum of itself. The cities blaze. The space between them
has gone dark and silent. Weather has simplified. Birds are a rumor children investigate.

**The player's arc in one line.** From instrument, to witness, to gardener with a gun.

# III. THE WORLD OF 2071

## III.1 The planet

Earth, damaged in a specific and legible way. The Grimwood Cycle does not burn forests; it
*drinks* them — extracting the metabolic charge of living tissue and leaving the structure
standing, grey, rigid, and dead. This single fact shapes the entire visual and ecological
world:

- **The Stillwoods.** Continental belts of harvested forest. Trees remain upright for
  decades — bark bleached to bone-grey, leaves fossilized mid-curl, entire canopies turned to
  brittle sculpture. Walking a stillwood is walking a cast of a forest. Nothing rots, because
  the decomposers were drunk along with everything else. It is the franchise's signature
  landscape: a dead world that still holds the *shape* of the living one.
- **The Quiet Belt.** The larger phenomenon: regions where the soil biome itself has been
  harvested. No insects, no birds, no soil turnover. Rain still falls but the land has
  forgotten what to do with it — sheet flooding, grey mudflats, mineral rivers running clear
  and lifeless as glass.
- **The Last Canopies.** Perhaps a dozen great living refugia remain: high-altitude cloud
  forests, a protected redwood coast (the setting of the reference board's forest scene),
  deep taiga too remote to concession profitably — yet. They are overwhelming to stand in
  now: the player should feel the *loudness* of life as something almost frightening after
  hours of stillwood silence.

## III.2 Geography of the Meridian Republic

- **Cadence** — the capital arcology. A poured-concrete megacity built vertically after the
  coastal retreats of the 2040s, grown around Edge Corporation's founding campus the way a
  pearl grows around grit. Estimated 40 million inhabitants. Home of **Steelgate**, Edge's
  headquarters complex — the site of the game's climactic battle (canon: reference board).
- **The Westward Concessions** — six administrative harvesting territories ceded by the
  Republic to Edge under the Concession Acts. Each is the size of a state. Concession III,
  "Amber," contains the redwood coast and is where Asher served.
- **The Furrows** — the agricultural machine-belt feeding the arcologies: kilometer-wide
  greenhouse vaults, the only legal green most citizens will ever see. Grown food is charged
  food; the Furrows are guarded like reactors.
- **Driftmark** — the salvage coast. Drowned pre-retreat suburbs, half-tide concrete, rust
  markets, smuggler harbors. Where the Sowers move seed and contraband.
- **The Provinces of the Interior** — depopulated small-town country inside the Quiet Belt.
  Emptied not by violence but by economics: when the land died, the towns followed. Standing
  houses, standing churches, standing grain elevators — all grey.

## III.3 Sky, weather, seasons

- **Night sky:** the cities wash it out; the Quiet Belt reveals it with pre-industrial
  clarity. Stars over the stillwood are described by travelers as "indecent" — too many, too
  bright, over land too dark. Orbital mirror-farms (Edge's supplemental solar) cross the sky
  as slow artificial constellations, and children in the provinces name them the way children
  once named star-pictures.
- **Weather:** simplified. With continental evapotranspiration collapsed, inland rain has
  weakened; the interior runs to long droughts broken by violent coastal storm intrusions.
  Dust from dead soil is the defining texture of provincial air — the "grey noons."
- **Seasons:** still occur, but muted in the Quiet Belt — nothing leafs, nothing turns,
  nothing falls. Edge's public calendars replaced seasonal festivals with quarterly "Charge
  Days." People notice. It is one of the small permanent griefs of the setting.

## III.4 What lives

- In the cities: humans, engineered pollinator drones, pigeon and rat populations (urban
  generalists survived), pet dogs and cats as status animals, rooftop moss gardens.
- In the canopies: everything, deafeningly.
- In the stillwood: almost nothing — and the "almost" is the mystery the franchise will mine
  for years. Rangers report **regrowth events**: single impossible saplings rising through
  the grey, always at sites where a Converter was destroyed violently. The Sowers treat these
  as proof and as scripture. Edge treats them as an accounting error. (Design note: this is
  the lore seed for the wave-survival gameplay — Edge sends machines to *re-harvest* regrowth
  sites, and defending a sapling against waves is the core combat fantasy.)

# IV. HISTORY — THE LONG TIMELINE

*The brief demands deep time, and deep time is what gives 2071 its weight. The megastructures
of the Meridian Republic are not sixty years old; they are the last chapter of a
seven-hundred-year architectural and moral lineage. The dates before 1900 are given in the
Republic's own historiography.*

## IV.1 The Deep Inheritance (c. 1300–1900)

The Meridian coast was settled, drowned, and resettled three times in its recorded history.
Out of that cycle came the region's two oldest institutions:

- **The Poured Tradition.** A school of flood-country building that answered water with mass:
  raised stone-and-lime platforms, then rammed earth, then — from the 1700s — early concretes.
  Its law was carved over the first surviving floodgate: *"WHAT STANDS, REMEMBERS."* Buildings
  were understood as the community's memory made load-bearing; to build lightly was to plan
  to forget. This is the ancestral root of the 2071 world's monumental concrete: the Gravity
  Style (Section VI) is the Poured Tradition with a corporate budget.
- **The Ledger.** The provinces' quiet folk faith (Section VIII): the belief that the world
  keeps a true account of what is taken from it and what is returned, and that every
  generation dies either in credit or in debt. Its clergy were called **Reckoners**; its
  sacrament was planting; its heresy was waste.

## IV.2 Industry and the First Spending (1900–2020)

Familiar history, told from 2071's vantage: coal, oil, the automobile century, the first
climate emergencies. The Republic's historians call this era **the First Spending** — the era
that taught the civilization its defining habit: *convert the stored past into the convenient
present, and call the difference growth.* The Grimwood Cycle will later be understood as the
First Spending's perfected, terminal form: no longer spending the *dead* past (fossils), but
the *living* present.

- 2031 in-world historiography marks **the Retreat Decade** (2030s–40s): coastal withdrawal,
  the poured megaprojects, the rise of arcology urbanism, and the political exhaustion that
  made the Republic willing to trade anything for a stable grid.

## IV.3 The Discovery (2031–2038)

**2031.** Dr. Beck Grimwood, a bioenergetics prodigy at the Cadence Institute, publishes the
paper that changes everything: *"On the Recoverable Charge of Living Systems."* The insight —
elegant, verifiable, Nobel-crowned — is that the energy a plant banks over years of
photosynthesis can be *withdrawn in minutes* if its metabolic pathways are collapsed in a
controlled cascade rather than burned. Orders of magnitude cleaner than combustion. No smoke,
no carbon pulse, no waste heat worth mentioning. The press calls it "harvest without fire."
The paper's final sentence becomes the most quoted and most damning line of the century:

> *"The biosphere is, among other things, a battery which has been charging for three billion
> years; it is time we learned to hold the terminals."*

**2034.** Grimwood, denied scale-up funding by a cautious Institute, accepts private backing
from a logistics conglomerate named **Edge Holdings**. He brings his prototype with him: a
shoulder-slung induction device the engineers nickname **the Converter** (canon: reference
board — boxy, rugged, green-lensed, deliberately *tool-like*, a chainsaw's soul in a
projector's body).

**2038.** The first commercial Bioharvest of a licensed timber tract. A crew of forty with
Converters and man-sized capacitor packs — **Bioharvest Vaults** (canon) — drains in one week
what a biomass plant would have burned in a year. Edge Holdings becomes **Edge Corporation**.
The stock chart from this year is later etched, floor-to-ceiling, in the lobby of Steelgate —
the first ascending line of a graph that will consume a hemisphere.

## IV.4 The Golden Decade (2038–2049)

It works. That is the tragedy — it *works*. Energy prices collapse. The last coal and gas
plants close a decade early; Edge markets the Cycle as the technology that *ended fossil
fuels*, and this is true, and the ads are beautiful, and the Republic's emissions hit zero in
2044 to global applause. Grids stabilize. The arcologies bloom with light. Medicine, transit,
desalination — everything electricity touches gets cheaper, which is everything.

The costs are distant, deferred, and voluntarily blind:

- Harvest quotas are set by Edge's own ecologists ("sustainable rotation").
- The first stillwoods appear behind fences, photographed by no one.
- The Ledger's Reckoners preach against "the Great Withdrawal" to shrinking congregations —
  who can argue with hospitals that never brown out?
- **2049.** The Republic, fiscally broken by the Retreat and politically addicted to cheap
  charge, signs the **First Concession Act**: sovereign harvesting rights over federal
  wildlands, ceded to Edge for ninety-nine years. Government remains the government —
  parliament sits, courts rule, flags fly — but on any question touching energy, the state
  has become a tenant in its own house. (Canon per user direction: the government and the
  corporation are separate; the government simply *let it happen*, and keeps letting it.)

## IV.5 The Quiet Creep (2050–2063)

The reference board's synopsis is set here: *"In 2050, the Republic relies on energy
converters that extract energy from plants, causing massive deforestation and environmental
collapse."*

- Harvest outpaces every projection. The rotation model fails silently: harvested land does
  not recover, because the Cycle takes the soil biome too. Edge's own scientists know by
  2053. The finding is filed, classified, and the filing itself becomes the century's
  greatest crime — not a massacre but a *memo*.
- The provinces empty. The Quiet Belt is named by a radio journalist who drives across the
  continent with a sound recorder and broadcasts forty minutes of nothing.
- Edge militarizes its Concessions: the **Wardenry** is founded — corporate enforcers in
  bone-white and safety-amber, tasked with "asset protection": anti-poaching, anti-sabotage,
  eviction of squatters, and escort of harvest crews. Asher Forester will be one of them.
- Grimwood, now Edge's Chief Scientist and a shareholder beyond counting, begins the
  **Devastator program** (canon): heavy autonomous harvest-and-defense platforms for
  "contested biomes" — the polite term for forests that people chain themselves to.

## IV.6 The Crack (2064–2070)

- **2064 — the Verdance Crash.** Concession II's yields collapse a decade ahead of schedule.
  Energy futures spike; Edge responds not with restraint but with *acceleration*: emergency
  harvesting of previously reserved canopies. The mask does not slip — it is *removed*,
  because the arithmetic no longer allows pretending.
- **2066.** The Sowers coalesce out of ruined ranger services, Ledger congregations, walked-off
  harvest crews, and one very good forger of Edge transit permits. Their method is not
  bombing; it is *planting* — nocturnal seed drops in drained Concessions, Converter thefts,
  and the slow perfection of running Grimwood's machine **in reverse** (Section VII.4). Their
  sign is a single green line breaking a grey circle.
- **2068.** The **Amber Incident** (Asher's story-root, canon-adjacent to the board's
  synopsis): during a night harvest in Concession III, a Devastator-2 misreads a work crew as
  intruders. Three workers die. Warden First Class Asher Forester, dragging a survivor clear,
  loses his left hand to the machine's cutting head. Edge settles quietly, fits him with the
  finest prosthetic in the catalog, promotes him, and classifies the log. The hand is
  excellent. It is also, forever, *theirs* — a piece of Edge grafted onto his body, humming
  faintly when the Converters run.
- **2069–70.** Asher's defection (Book II will detail it; the board gives the shape): the
  gift of a living plant from a critical friend — a small green thing in a world that had
  made green a unit of account — the months of doubt, the leaked soil-biome memo, and the
  night he walks out of a Wardenry barracks with a duffel bag, a service weapon, and the
  prosthetic they will never stop being able to track.

## IV.7 The Present (2071)

The game begins here. Edge announces **Project Evergreen** — publicly, a "closed-loop
photosynthesis farm" that will end wild harvesting forever; privately (the demo's central
secret), the industrialization of Grimwood's final work: a Converter scaled to drink a
*living canopy in whole* — the redwood coast, the continent's last great green. The Sowers
learn the target. Asher knows the ground, the protocols, the Wardenry's rotations, and the
way into Steelgate.

History has narrowed to a door, and he is the one holding the keys he was never supposed to
keep.

# V. THE CIVILIZATION OF THE ACCORD AGE

*"The Accord Age" is what Edge's own historians call 2049–present: the era of the Concession
Accords. The Sowers call the same years "the Withdrawal." Both are correct.*

## V.1 How people live

**In the arcologies (85% of the population):** densely, vertically, comfortably, and under
glass. A Cadence resident's life is warm, lit, medically excellent, and almost entirely
interior. Parks are subscription spaces — real grass, real trees, entry metered by the
minute, the hum of the humidity plant always audible under the birdsong recording. The
average citizen touches unmanaged nature zero times in their life and is not especially
bothered; this absence-of-bother is the moral weather of the setting.

**In the provinces (the rest):** sparsely, in the towns the Quiet Belt is slowly
mummifying. Provincials live by salvage, Furrow-contract labor, smuggling, and stubbornness.
They are not romanticized — the setting resists the "noble countryside" cliché — but they
remember things arcology citizens have outsourced: weather, darkness, the names of trees.

**Food:** Furrow-grown, nutritionally complete, aesthetically managed. Real outdoor-grown
food ("dirt food") is a black-market luxury and a Sower fundraising staple. The taste
difference is small. People pay for it anyway, for reasons they find hard to say aloud.

**Family and time:** small families, late children, long lives. The Accord Age is not a
dystopia of jackboots; it is a dystopia of *comfort on credit*, and its citizens are us,
slightly further down the corridor.

## V.2 What they value, what they fear

- **Valued:** continuity, competence, quiet, "clean" (the era's highest compliment — clean
  energy, clean lines, clean records). Waste is the residual sin the Ledger left in the
  culture: even arcology dwellers who have never prayed sort their recycling with liturgical
  seriousness, a vestigial organ of an older morality.
- **Feared:** the dark grid. Every citizen has lived through one Charge Emergency — 2064's
  rolling blackouts — and the memory functions the way plague-memory once did. Edge's
  propaganda does not need to invent fear; it needs only to schedule the reminder.
- **Unspoken:** the Quiet itself. Arcology media simply does not show the stillwoods. There
  is no censorship law; there is something stronger — a shared, unexamined agreement not to
  look. The game's cinematography breaks that agreement on the player's behalf.

## V.3 Education, work, art of daily life

Education is corporate-tracked from adolescence (an "Edge Scholar" pin at fourteen is a
family's pride and its mortgage). Work is abundant — the Accord Age runs on maintenance,
logistics, and care work; harvest labor is the one job always hiring and always avoided.
Public art is memorial by instinct: the arcologies are full of *pressed-leaf reliquaries* —
glass-cased botanical specimens in lobbies and stations, labeled like saints' relics
(*QUERCUS ALBA, CONCESSION I, WITHDRAWN 2051*). Nobody calls them tombstones. Everybody
understands them to be tombstones.

# VI. THE ARCHITECTURAL LANGUAGE — THE GRAVITY STYLE

*Architecture is the second protagonist of this franchise. The reference board's concrete
canyon — light falling between cyclopean fins onto figures the size of punctuation — is the
target image for every environment artist. This section is the rulebook behind that image.*

## VI.1 Why everything is enormous and why everything is concrete

Three converging causes, all historically honest:

1. **The Poured Inheritance.** Seven centuries of flood-country building taught the Meridian
   coast that *mass is memory* — "what stands, remembers." Concrete is the civilization's
   mother material, its marble and its scripture.
2. **The Retreat.** The 2030s–40s coastal withdrawal demanded shelter for tens of millions in
   a decade. Poured megastructure was the only construction method that scaled. An entire
   generation of architects learned exactly one style at exactly planetary scale.
3. **Cheap charge.** After 2038, energy cost nothing — and concrete is congealed energy.
   Edge *subsidized* monumental construction as marketing: every kilometer-long turbine hall
   is an advertisement reading *this is what the harvest buys*. The stillwoods paid for the
   cathedrals. That sentence is the whole style.

## VI.2 The rules (for environment artists)

- **Scale is theological.** The human figure occupies less than 3% of any establishing
  frame's height. Doors are triple-height minimum in Edge structures; corridors are naves.
  The player must feel *processed* by buildings, not housed.
- **Repetition is rhetoric.** Fins, coffers, columns, and bays repeat past the point of
  usefulness — rhythm as power. Where a structure needs four supports, the Gravity Style
  pours forty. (The subliminal message, everywhere: *we do not run out.*)
- **Light is rationed, never decorative.** One monumental aperture per space. Interiors are
  designed around a single falling blade or pool of daylight (the board's canyon shot),
  everything else in deep grey shadow. Artificial light is warm, low, and horizontal
  (signage, striplights, ember tones) — never overhead floods. The visual grammar: *daylight
  = judgment; striplight = commerce.*
- **Surfaces carry their history.** Board-form texture, pour-lines like geological strata,
  rust bleeding from tie-holes, water staining in long vertical tongues. Every wall should
  answer the question "how was I made and how long have I stood?"
- **Emptiness is intentional.** The Accord Age over-built on cheap charge and then the
  provinces emptied; the Republic owns twice the enclosed volume it can inhabit. Vacancy is
  the default condition of any interior the player enters. Furniture is sparse, isolated,
  arranged like museum exhibits of habitation (the red-carpet island in a dark hall — canon
  image from the legacy prototype, retained).
- **Ideology is legible in section.** Edge buildings *descend* — their mass is below grade or
  looms overhead; power is expressed as weight above the visitor. Ledger structures *stand* —
  simple vertical halls, human doors, one tree in the court. Sower spaces *borrow* — they nest
  inside the leftover volume of both, plywood and cable inside concrete, warm lamps in cold
  naves.
- **District differentiation:**
  - *Steelgate / Edge civic:* polished pale grey, safety-amber accents, glyph signage,
    knife-edged formwork. Immaculate.
  - *Arcology residential:* the same grammar softened — warmer aggregate, moss planters,
    laundry lines strung between monumental fins (people domesticate anything).
  - *Concession infrastructure:* raw industrial pour, chevron warning paint, harvest gantries
    like cathedral organ-pipes aimed at the ground.
  - *Provincial/Quiet Belt:* pre-Accord human-scale brick and timber, grey-dusted, standing in
    the shadow of Accord viaducts that stride over the towns without touching them.
- **The stillwood as architecture.** Treat dead forests as buildings: trunks are columns,
  the canopy is a coffered ceiling, shafts of grey light fall as they do in the concrete
  halls. The two signature spaces of the game — turbine hall and stillwood — must rhyme.
  That rhyme *is* the theme stated visually: the civilization rebuilt the forest in concrete
  and called it progress.

## VI.3 Psychology of space (level-design intent)

Gravity Style interiors are sequenced to produce, in order: awe → smallness → unease →
(rarely, deliberately) shelter. "Shelter" beats — a Sower lamp-lit nest, a Ledger hall, the
green riot of a canopy — are rationed like the daylight, so the player's nervous system
learns the world's own hierarchy: comfort is scarce, engineered, and worth fighting for.

# VII. TECHNOLOGY

*Grounded, near-term, no magic. Every device is an extrapolation of the Grimwood Cycle plus
2070s-plausible engineering.*

## VII.1 The Grimwood Cycle (the one big lie of physics — kept small and consistent)

The franchise's single speculative license: living tissue's metabolic energy can be
catastrophically "cascaded" and captured inductively at high efficiency. Everything else
obeys ordinary physics. Rules, fixed forever:

1. Only *living* systems hold verdance. Dead biomass is worthless to a Converter — hence the
   stillwoods are not re-harvestable, and hence living canopies become the scarcest strategic
   resource on Earth.
2. Yield scales with accumulated growth: a three-hundred-year redwood is a *reservoir*; a
   lawn is pocket change. (This is why old growth dies first and why Project Evergreen
   targets the last of it.)
3. The cascade is directional but not, Grimwood's suppressed 2053 paper proved,
   *irreversible*: charge can be driven back into compatible living tissue to accelerate
   growth — at ruinous efficiency, tens of units spent per unit banked. Economically absurd.
   Ecologically: a seed. This is the Sowers' stolen sacrament (VII.4).

## VII.2 The Converter (canon prop)

Shoulder-slung induction projector; the reference board's design is final (boxy chassis,
green condenser lens, red capacitor spine, rust-worn edge protectors — a *tool*, not a gun,
which is precisely why it is horrifying). Operator doctrine: plant the stabilizer, sight the
trunk, hold the cascade until the lens goes dark. A veteran crew drains a hectare a shift.
The sound design is canonical: not a roar but a *sigh* — the forest's charge leaving as a
long descending chord (the audio team's most important single asset).

**In-game:** the Converter is also Asher's story-weapon — a stolen unit re-governed by Sower
engineers. Modes are diegetic re-purposings, not sci-fi guns: cascade-lash (short-range
discharge), capacitor slam (area shove), and the reverse-cycle "Sower's Charge" that heals
allied regrowth. Book V (weapons) will detail; Book I fixes only the fiction.

## VII.3 The machines of Edge

- **Bioharvest Vaults** (canon): man-sized capacitor packs, the era's ubiquitous heavy
  object — carried, stacked, convoyed, stolen. A full Vault powers a city block for a month
  or a Devastator for a week; in the demo they are objectives, cover, and (ruptured)
  area-denial hazards. Visual: the board's design — drum body, teal charge-lines, red
  service latches.
- **The Devastator series** (canon): Grimwood's "contested biome" platforms. D-1 (2058): a
  harvest gantry with a temper. D-2 (2065): the Amber Incident's machine — quadruped
  siege-harvester, cutting heads, red visor band. D-3 "3000-series" (2070, the board's red
  giant): a walking refinery with integrated Converter array and anti-personnel suite,
  deployed at Steelgate and at Evergreen sites. Design language: industrial, blocky,
  safety-liveried — Edge paints its violence in the same amber-and-white as its forklifts,
  because to Edge it *is* a forklift.
- **Wardenry kit:** ballistic weapons remain the era's sidearm truth (the board's white/grey
  pistols and carbines with amber charge-windows) — verdance powers grids, not handguns.
  Wardens wear bone-white composite with amber signal panels; hazard crews the yellow suits
  of the canyon image.
- **Pollinator and survey drones, mirror-farms, prosthetics** (Asher's hand: Edge
  "Meridian-pattern" myoelectric, ivory shell, amber status ring — narratively, a tracking
  liability and the story's ticking motif), arcology climate plants, mag-freight. All
  plausible, all maintained, nothing "smart" enough to want anything. **No AGI in this
  universe, ever** — the machines are exactly as moral as their owners, which is the point.

## VII.4 The Sowers' counter-technology

Reverse-cycled Converters ("green units" — lens burns verdant instead of harvest-white),
seed-case mortars that plant instead of shatter, Vault leeching rigs, and the quiet craft of
*grafting* — splicing living cuttings onto stillwood roots at regrowth sites. Sower tech
looks like Edge tech field-stripped, re-wired, and carried too far by too few people:
electrical tape, hand-painted green line, prayer.

# VIII. RELIGION

## VIII.1 The Ledger (the old faith)

Born in the flood centuries alongside the Poured Tradition, the Ledger is less a church than
a *bookkeeping of being*. Its creation story is an audit: the world was not made, it was
*lent* — every living thing arrives owing its substance and dies having either returned more
than it drew (grace) or less (debt). There is no heaven; there is the **Standing Balance**,
the sum of all lives, which every soul nudges green or grey.

- **Clergy:** Reckoners — part accountant, part arborist, part funeral officiant. They carry
  bound paper ledgers (paper, always; "what is written in charge can be unwritten").
- **Sacraments:** planting (birth), grafting (marriage — two lines joined on one rootstock),
  and the **Returning** (funeral): burial unembalmed beneath a sapling, the grave-tree
  recorded in the parish ledger with the deceased's name. A Ledger cemetery is an orchard of
  names. The stillwooding of a parish orchard is, to believers, a second death of everyone
  in it — this, not economics, is why the provinces hate Edge with a grief that looks like
  religion, because it is.
- **Architecture:** the Standing Halls — plain vertical naves of poured lime-concrete, one
  human-scaled door, one roof aperture over one living tree in the floor. The tree is the
  altar. A hall whose tree has died is deconsecrated and left open to the sky.
- **Heresy:** *waste* — spending life without return. The Ledger never forbade harvest; it
  forbade harvest without planting. Grimwood was raised Ledger. Book II will make much of
  this: the Cycle is a Reckoner's son's discovery — withdrawal perfected, return abandoned.
- **Relation to technology:** unbothered by machines as such ("a tool is a hand's promise"),
  implacable on the Cycle ("the first tool that lies").

## VIII.2 The Bright Hour (the corporate-age cult)

The Accord Age's own faith, grown in the arcologies among people who needed absolution more
than doctrine. The Bright Hour teaches that the harvest is a *translation*, not a death: the
forests are not gone, they are **in the grid** — every lit window a leaf, every warm room a
bloom. Its liturgy is the evening switch-on; its temple is any view of the skyline at dusk;
its symbol is a white circle rayed with filament lines (Edge's marketing department did not
invent it, but funds it with a discretion bordering on tenderness).

It is not a joke faith. Its believers are sincere, kind, and numerous — nurses, teachers,
grid engineers — people who keep the arcologies alive and cannot afford to believe they do so
by candlelight of a burning library. The Bright Hour is the anesthesia of the Accord Age,
and the game treats its believers with pity, never contempt. Its quiet crisis: Bright Hour
chapels keep a "translation register" of harvested tracts — and some registrars, copying
Edge's manifests night after night, do the arithmetic, and walk to the Sowers carrying the
most dangerous thing in the Republic: accurate numbers.

# IX. POLITICS

## IX.1 The Meridian Republic — a state that let go

Canon principle (user direction): the government is *separate* from Edge and *let it
happen*. The Republic is a genuine parliamentary federation — elections occur, courts
function, the press is free in law. But the Concession Acts created a second, deeper
constitution: whoever schedules the charge schedules the country.

- **The Green Courts:** the judiciary's environmental bench — once toothless, now the last
  legal front. Sower lawyers (they exist; not every Sower carries a seed mortar) fight
  Concession renewals tract by tract. Edge has never lost a case and never once relaxed,
  because a single loss creates precedent, and precedent is the one thing money cannot
  un-plant.
- **The Chartered Assembly:** parliament. A third of its members sit in Edge-funded seats in
  everything but name. Another third would vote against Edge if voting against Edge did not
  read, to their constituents, as voting for darkness. The final third writes very good
  speeches.
- **The Wardenry problem:** legally, Edge's enforcers are "asset protection contractors."
  Practically, they are the largest armed force on the continent. The Republic's army is
  smaller, older, and — after the 2064 Charge Emergency, when Edge kept the hospitals lit —
  emotionally garrisoned to Edge's side.

## IX.2 Propaganda and the management of sight

Edge's genius is curatorial, not censorial. Nothing is banned; attention is simply
*designed*. Concession flyover routes are charted around stillwood; arcology school
"nature units" teach the Furrows; the harvest is shown constantly — as time-lapse renewal
footage, replanting montages (real footage: demonstration plots, 0.4% of withdrawal, the
Republic's most-filmed hectares). The literal truth of every individual frame, assembled
into a comprehensive lie: this is the Edge house style, in media as in law.

## IX.3 Reform, revolt, and the road not taken

- **2057, the Rotation Reform:** the Republic's one real attempt to legislate harvest caps.
  Passed. Celebrated. Quietly gutted in committee-defined "measurement standards" written by
  Edge secondees. The Reform's author retired to the provinces and now runs a Sower seedbank;
  Book II may knock on her door.
- **The Sowers' constitutional theory:** they do not consider themselves revolutionaries.
  Their founding document, *The Standing Claim*, argues the Concessions are void — you cannot
  cede what you do not own, and no generation owns the living world. "We are not breaking the
  law. We are enforcing an older one." The Republic's response to this argument is the
  Wardenry.

# X. LANGUAGE, WRITING, AND MARKINGS

*The world's graphic voice. Everything here is buildable as decals, signage kits, and UI.*

## X.1 The two alphabets

- **Civic Meridian:** the Republic writes in a familiar Latin-descended script. Typography is
  the era's class marker: the state and the old institutions set text in **monumental
  editorial serif** (the reference board's poster face — high-contrast, carved, museum-plate
  authority: used in-game for chapter cards, location titles, memorial inscriptions).
  Commerce and Edge set text in **industrial grotesk** (flat, wide, wayfinding-neutral: used
  for HUD, signage, machine labels).
- **Chargehand:** the harvest industry's field shorthand — a stencil glyph system evolved for
  spray-marking timber, Vaults, and routes. ~60 glyphs: tract-claimed, drained, unstable,
  live-line, crew-cache, "reckoned" (the Sowers' countermark — a green line struck through
  the drained-glyph). Environment artists: Chargehand is the game's environmental
  storytelling font; a player who learns six glyphs can read any corridor's history.

## X.2 Recurring words (the era's vocabulary)

*Verdance* (living charge) · *withdrawal* (harvest, official) · *drinking* (harvest, worker
slang) · *stillwood / the grey* · *charge-poor / charge-blind* (provincial poverty) · *dirt
food* · *the Quiet* · *reckoned* (Sower: replanted/avenged — the double meaning is the point)
· *grey noon* (dust weather) · *translation* (Bright Hour euphemism for harvest) · *the
sigh* (sound of a Converter cascade).

## X.3 Numbers, insignia, warnings

Edge numbers everything in **tract-serials** (`C3-AMBER-0447`) stenciled in grotesk;
Devastators carry unit chevrons and a red service band (never eyes — Edge paints machines as
appliances). Republic insignia: a poured arch over water. Edge's mark: an ascending edge-line
breaking a circle's rim (the graph, abstracted; employees call it "the sunrise" without
irony). Warning liveries: harvest amber (chevroned), charge teal (Vault lines), quarantine
grey (stillwood boundary — the only sign in the Republic printed in serif, because the
boundary office predates Edge; a detail for the attentive).

# XI. ART IN THE ACCORD AGE

Memorial by reflex, monumental by inheritance. Painting has drifted into **greyfield
work** — vast near-monochrome canvases of stillwood horizons (the arcology rich hang what
the arcology poor fled). Sculpture is civic and cyclopean, cast in the same pours as the
architecture. Music: the concert tradition endures (charge is cheap; halls are lit), but the
era's defining form is **field recording** — pre-Quiet soundscapes traded like paintings;
a 2040s dawn chorus in original fidelity auctions like an old master. Theater survives in
the provinces as Ledger cantatas — sung audits of a town's standing balance, half mass, half
town meeting.

And the **graphic novel** is the age's confession booth: cheap, printable, smuggleable, the
one medium Edge's curation never captured. Provincial pressings — silhouette-heavy,
two-color (grey + one green), serif-titled — carry the Quiet's images past the arcology
attention design. **This is the game's cutscene form** (canon for Book VIII): the story's
cinematics are pressed pages of an in-world banned graphic novel, "ECHOES OF EDEN," drawn by
an anonymous Sower artist. The player is literally reading the resistance's own record.

---

# XII. THE COLLAPSE — THE QUIET

*Not an event. A tide table.*

**What happened:** Sections IV.4–IV.6 in sequence — discovery, golden decade, silent
rotation failure, the 2053 memo, acceleration after the 2064 Crash. No single decision was
mad. The sum was.

**Why nobody stopped it:** everyone who could was paid in the harvest's currency; everyone
who couldn't was warmed by it. The Ledger preached to shrinking rooms because the rooms Edge
lit were larger. The scientists who knew wrote memos; the memo-readers had mortgages in
Cadence. The truth was never hidden well — it was simply *outshone*.

**Who benefited:** everyone, briefly; then fewer, greatly. The Accord Age's prosperity was
real — that must never be softened, or the story becomes a cartoon. Edge's shareholders and
the arcology professional class still live inside the benefit. The provinces paid first;
the future pays the balance.

**Who resisted:** Reckoners, rangers, walked-off crews, the Rotation Reform's authors, and
finally the Sowers — resistance evolving from sermon to lawsuit to seed.

**How people adapted:** they moved inward and upward; they subscribed to parks; they hung
pressed leaves in lobbies; they stopped showing their children pictures of the grey, then
stopped looking themselves; they built the Bright Hour so the lights could mean something.
Adaptation, the game insists, is the collapse's final stage — the moment the wound is
renamed the weather.

**How nature answers:** the regrowth events (III.4). The world, it turns out, keeps its own
ledger, and somewhere in the arithmetic of the reversed Cycle it has begun, very quietly, to
collect.

---

# XIII. THE PRESENT — 2071 AT STREET LEVEL

What remains: the entire built world, over-poured and under-inhabited. Functioning: the
grid, the Furrows, the mag-lines, the Wardenry, the courts, the myth of normalcy.
Forgotten: birdsong (licensed recordings excepted), seasons in the Quiet Belt, the names of
common trees (arcology children can identify a redwood; it is on the money). Mythologized:
the regrowth events ("green ghosts"), the Sower artist of *Echoes of Eden*, and Grimwood
himself — half Prometheus, half Faust, unseen publicly since 2069, at work on Evergreen
behind Steelgate's deepest pour.

The stage, as the demo opens: Evergreen announced; the redwood coast surveyed; a Sower cell
short one insider — and an ex-Warden with an ivory hand walking out of the grey to volunteer.

# XIV. FACTIONS

## XIV.1 Edge Corporation
History: IV.3–IV.7. Philosophy: continuity at any cost — *"the lights stay on."* Appearance:
bone-white, pale grey, safety amber; immaculate liveries; violence dressed as maintenance.
Architecture: Gravity Style at its coldest. Morality: institutional — no mustache-twirling;
every Edge hand the player fights believes, correctly, that millions depend on the grid he
defends. Leadership: a Board the public never sees; Grimwood as sanctified founder-mind;
**Director Halden Voss** (new canon, Book II) as the operational face — the man who signs
what Grimwood dreams. Conflicts: yields vs. time, secrecy vs. scale, and one defected Warden
who knows the corridor schedules.

## XIV.2 The Wardenry
Edge's enforcement arm and Asher's old family — the game's human enemy faction, written with
the loyalty-culture of people who mostly wanted steady work and a crew. Bone-white composite,
amber panels, yellow hazard variants. Their creed, stenciled in every barracks: *"BETWEEN
THE WORK AND HARM."* They believe it. That is the tragedy the combat design must carry.

## XIV.3 The Sowers
Founded 2066 (IV.6). Philosophy: *The Standing Claim* — enforcement of the older law.
Method: plant first, fight when found. Appearance: salvage-technical — Edge castoffs
re-lined, wax-canvas green, the single hand-painted line. Architecture: nests inside the
leftover world. Morality: fierce, funerary, allergic to martyrdom-cult ("a dead Sower plants
nothing"). Leadership: cellular; the demo's cell is led by **Mara Okafor** (new canon — the
"critical friend" of the board's synopsis, the one who gave Asher the plant). Conflicts:
pacifist-planter wing vs. the demolition wing; both vs. the clock Evergreen has started.

## XIV.4 The Ledger
Faith, not army (VIII.1) — but its parishes are the Sowers' sea: seedbanks in Standing
Halls, Reckoners as couriers, orchards as dead-drops. Its moral authority is the one
resource Edge cannot buy at any price, and Edge knows it: Wardens are standing-ordered
never to enter a Hall armed. The order holds until, in the demo's second act, it doesn't —
Edge's first unforgivable *image*, and the story's turning of public sight.

## XIV.5 The Mulchers
Salvage syndicates of Driftmark and the Belt: stillwood loggers (dead timber is free
timber), Vault-strippers, permit-forgers, nobody's heroes. They sell to Sowers and inform to
Wardens, sometimes in the same week. Narrative function: the world's grey market and the
demo's neutral zone — proof the Quiet has an economy, because everything does.

## XIV.6 The Registry (wildcard)
Bright Hour registrars and Republic archivists who copy the harvest's true numbers —
believers whose faith requires accurate books. Not a resistance; a *record*. The franchise's
long game: when the Republic finally turns, it will turn on the Registry's arithmetic, not
on gunfire. Seeds of that turn are planted in the demo as collectible documents.

# XV. SYMBOLS (RECURRING, WITH REASONS)

- **The circle broken by a line.** Edge's sunrise-graph mark — and the Sowers' inversion
  (green line breaking a grey circle: growth escaping enclosure). One geometry, two futures;
  the game's logo dialectic (canon: matches the board's title treatments).
- **The single tree in an aperture of light.** Ledger altar, regrowth site, the plant on
  Asher's windowsill: life as the rationed light's one tenant. Level designers: whenever a
  space earns its single daylight shaft, ask what stands in it.
- **The ledger line / double rule.** Accounting's "balance struck" mark — carved on Standing
  Hall lintels, painted under Sower victories, and the game's save icon.
- **The sigh-chord (audio symbol).** The Converter's descending cascade tone; its reversal
  (ascending, when the Sowers' green units run) is the hope-motif of the entire score.
- **Amber.** The color of warning and of wages — Edge's honesty, in livery form.
- **The ivory hand.** Asher's prosthetic: gift, leash, receipt. The story in one prop.

# XVI. VISUAL LANGUAGE (LOCKED TO THE REFERENCE BOARD)

- **Palette:** concrete greys (60%), bone-white and safety amber (Edge, 15%), deep shadow
  (15%), one living green (10% — *rationed*; green is the frame's currency and must be spent
  like it). Red is reserved for Devastator presence and blood-adjacent alarm.
- **Light:** single-source monumental daylight; horizontal warm striplight; the grey noon.
  Never two hero lights in one frame.
- **Scale:** megalophobic framing per VI.2 — humans as punctuation (canon image: the yellow
  crews in the concrete canyon).
- **Texture:** board-form concrete, rust bleed, wax canvas, worn composite; grain and subtle
  chromatic lens per the established filmic stack.
- **Figures:** silhouette-first (the board's ideation sheets): Wardens read as pale verticals
  with amber breaks; Sowers as soft-shouldered bundles; Grimwood as a split silhouette —
  clean coat, ruined mechanical arm; the Devastator-3000 as a red monolith with legs.
- **Cutscenes:** the pressed-page graphic-novel form (XI), two-color, serif-titled, held
  frames with parallax — *stillness as style*, motion rationed like green.

# XVII. TONE — THE FIVE PILLARS

1. **Awe before anger.** The world is monumental first, unjust second; the player's smallness
   is the argument.
2. **No cartoons.** Edge believes itself; the Wardenry loves its own; the Bright Hour is
   sincere. The horror is that everyone is doing their job.
3. **Green is sacred and scarce** — in the palette, the economy, the level design, the score.
4. **Hope is manual labor.** Nothing regrows by speech; every hopeful beat is someone
   physically planting, grafting, carrying, holding a wave line at a sapling.
5. **The quiet is the antagonist.** Silence is designed, diegetic, and spreading; the final
   boss of the franchise, behind every Devastator, is the sound going out of the world.
